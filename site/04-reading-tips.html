Reading Tips for Non-Voice-Actors

You are not a voice actor. Good news: you don't have to be. Listeners do not expect a Broadway performance from a self-narrated book. They expect a warm, clear voice reading words the author clearly cares about — and that's something you can do from day one.

The trick is not "sounding like a narrator." The trick is sounding like a person who is talking to one friend.

The one-friend rule

Imagine one specific person — a friend who would love your book — and read to them, not into the room. Do this literally: put a photo of a friend near the mic, or hang a sticky note with their name on the blanket fort. Everything else in this chapter is downstream of this.

Practical framing:

  • Talk slightly slower than conversation, not "narrator slow".
  • Speak at 90% of your conversational volume — energy without strain.
  • Let sentences land. Don't rush the end of a sentence to get to the next one.

Breathing: the forgotten foundation

  • Breathe from your belly. Your shoulders should stay still.
  • Breathe before thinking, not mid-sentence. Read a phrase, choose where you'll breathe next (usually at commas and periods), then breathe there. If a sentence is long, take a mid-sentence breath at a comma — that's what commas are for.
  • If you find yourself running out of air, your mic is probably too far or you're speaking too loudly. Close in an inch and drop your volume.

Mouth noises: the small enemy

Dry lips and a dry mouth make clicks and smacks — the most annoying thing about unedited amateur audio. Your pre-session ritual:

  1. Room-temperature water, sipped often. Never ice, never dairy right before recording.
  2. A green apple (seriously): the bite and acid neutralize sticky mouth sound better than anything. One apple covers about 90 minutes.
  3. No coffee in the last 20 minutes if you're prone to clicks (for some people it helps instead — experiment once and remember your result).
  4. A gentle pre-reading lip/tongue stretch, and one soft hum to warm the voice. Two minutes, tops.

These little things carry enormous weight over a full book.

Voice hygiene, honestly

Your voice will tire differently than normal conversation does. Helpful, not-iconic advice:

  • 90 minutes is roughly a session. After that your voice tires in ways you won't hear until editing — and tired voice makes you push, which sounds strained. Take a real break, or stop for the day.
  • Never "push through" soreness. A hoarse voice in your audiobook is a permanently hoarse audiobook. Cancel the session, drink water, come back tomorrow. Your deadline is yours.
  • If your voice cracks, do note the timestamp and keep reading. Editing (Chapter 5) handles pick-ups easily. Don't restart a chapter for one crack unless it's dramatic — that's how a whole afternoon disappears.

Stumbles: how to handle them live

You will flub words. Here is the cheap, pro move used by every voice actor:

On any flub: pause for one full second, then back up to the start of the phrase (not just the single word) and re-read it cleanly, then continue normally.

Because every flub is bracketed by clear pauses, you (or an editor — even a free one you hire per-chapter) can find and fix them like turning pages. One-second pause, re-read, continue. Nothing more exiting.

Do not say "sorry" or sigh dramatically or make a remark — the editor-you has to cut all that later for no gain.

Pacing and energy across a whole book

Over a novel-length recording, two things quietly drift without you noticing:

  • Pacing: by chapter 9 you'll be reading 15% faster than chapter 1. Fix: listen to 30 seconds of your first chapter before every session to recalibrate your ear.
  • Energy: the "reading glasses voice" creeps in after an hour. Fix: the one-friend rule; remind yourself who they are between chapters.

For non-fiction: give lists and steps a tiny upward inflection on the sentence-leading words so the listener hears structure coming, not just a wall of sentences.

For fiction: don't over-act dialogue. Two characters flirting barely needs two "voices"; it needs two senses of rhythm. The cheapest trick: tilt your head slightly or change your sitting posture for a new speaker. That tiny physical change tends to shift your voice naturally — no "voice acting" required.

Handling your text on the desk

  • Print the manuscript, one chapter at a time, with 1.5-inch margins and 13–14 pt font for notes. Paper doesn't scroll, buzz, or glow.
  • Read a chapter once aloud (silently, in your head) before recording it. Ten minutes of pre-reading kills 90% of flubs.
  • Pencil in emphasis: circle words you must punch, arrows where tone rises/falls, double-underlines for anything with unusual pronunciation (names, jargon) and write your chosen pronunciation right there.
  • Keep a "replaceable words" sticky list of names/terms you often flub, so you'll see it before recording a new chapter.

The last mile: fatigue, self-doubt, momentum

Two honest feelings to expect, and what to do with them:

  • "Do I sound like me?" Yes; you're hearing your own voice through a different channel for the first time at high resolution — nobody else hears you the way you do, and this strangeness fades in a week of sessions. Do not re-record entire chapters because of it.
  • "This will take forever." It will take hours, not forever — about three to six recorded hours per finished hour for a beginner, dropping fast. A 60,000-word book is around 7 finished hours; most authors finish in a few weeks of casual evening sessions.

You will improve noticeably by chapter three, and nobody listening to the final book will know which chapters came first. Keep recording.
