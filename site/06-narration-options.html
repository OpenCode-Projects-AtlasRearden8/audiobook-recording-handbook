Your Three Narration Routes: DIY, Hire, or AI

Now the big strategic question. You have three honest routes to getting a finished audiobook:

  1. Record it yourself (the rest of this handbook — the working route)
  2. Hire a narrator (the money route)
  3. Use free AI narration (the trade-off route)

This chapter lays out the real costs, timelines, control, and limits of each — including which listener-facing doors each route opens and closes. No hype, no guilt. Some books belong to each route.

Route 1: DIY narration

Working through Chapters 1–6 of this handbook. Your total cash cost: $40–$120 of gear you may already own. Your total time cost: roughly 2–5 hours per finished hour of audio at beginner speed, improving fast.

Pros

  • Control. Your voice, your pacing, your emphasis, your pronunciations. Perfect for memoir, business books where the author is the story, and anything where "the author reading her own book" is part of the value.
  • Cost. Free-ish forever; every future audiobook gets easier.
  • Selling floor. DIY narration is accepted everywhere, including full acceptance into ACX/Audible royalty pool — as long as the technical specs from Chapter 3 are met, first-person quality doesn't matter to checklist requirements.
  • A skill you keep. Every future audiobook gets faster and better.

Cons

  • It's a lot of hours, and they're solo focused-work hours — this is a real cost if you have ADHD, caregiving, or a full-time job.
  • Voice drag: everyone's opinion of their own voice starts negative. (Chapter 4: this fades.)
  • If your health/voice/schedule is unpredictable, a whole-book deadline becomes a soft one. Elapsed time is the real risk, not quality.

DIY vs the pros, honestly

Roughly: DIY works well if either (a) you want to read it and think the listening experience benefits from it being you, or (b) your budget is under a thousand dollars and the book will sell better with an audiobook than without, even imperfectly. DIY fails if you hate your voice so much you'd rather listen to silence than finish it — then the next two routes exist.

Route 2: Hiring a narrator

This route is old, well-understood, and does produce lovely books.

The money, in plain numbers

The industry standard price is Per Finished Hour (PFH):

  • $150–$300 PFH for a competent narrator on marketplaces like Upwork or Voices — roughly $1,000–$2,500 for a 7-hour nonfiction book.
  • $50–$125 PFH for a beginner narrator building a portfolio, or a narrow audiobook niche (short how-to, self-help with simple script) can land $400–$900.
  • $250–$400+ PFH for experienced narrators with sizable back catalogs (often booked direct; heavy demand).
  • Royalty-share (an ACX payment model, for US-eligible titles): you pay nothing up front, and the narrator takes half of your future royalties. It comes with ACX's standard 90-day exclusivity-to-Audible contract, and most experienced narrators decline it for unknown titles — expect newer or portfolio-building narrators to say yes.

7.5 finished hours was about $1,500–$2,000 in recent marketplace median. Budget accordingly or negotiate these alternatives:

  • Short books (20k words): hard to narrate cheaply per-hour. Some narrators do per-chapter or per-word rates for these.
  • Script-editing fees: most narrators charge more if a manuscript is heavy with names, tables, or code. Clean it for them (Chapter 4 pre-reading techniques exist exactly for this).

Pros

  • Beautiful sound without owning any problem the pros have.
  • Your time back: basically zero hours for you beyond approving.
  • Unlocked: full ACX store acceptance, DLBA (Findaway/Spotify retail), publishing via any wide distributor.

Cons

  • It's real money, up front, with zero guarantee of sales payback. A $2,000 audiobook needs a lot of listens before it is "worth it".
  • You lose control of voice and pacing. Narrators generally do a great job, but your memoir reads like their performance. (Fixable with a good brief; partially.)
  • Timeline: professional turnaround is usually 3–8 weeks including corrections rounds.

When to hire a narrator instead

If a polished audiobook matters more for a title's positioning than its per-unit earnings (for example, a flagship nonfiction title where the audiobook itself is a product feature, or your audience already knows they want to listen rather than read) — go ahead and hire. Otherwise, Route 1 (DIY) or Route 3 (AI) are genuinely on the table.

Route 3: Free (and cheap) AI narration

The actual options, in plain terms, as of writing. This section is the most likely of this whole chapter to age — AI narration options change monthly. Treat it as a field guide, not gospel, and check each platform's current page before you decide.

The genuinely free options

  1. Google Play Books auto-narration Google offers automated voice narration for eligible self-published books listed with the Google Play Books Partner Center. Currently:
    • Real pros: it's free and it's accepted by Google's own store. Your book can be sold with an AI voice with essentially just waiting.
    • Quality is "clearly synthesized but pleasant and steady". Nonfiction listens fine (a how-to, a guide, a manual like the one this handbook itself publishes as). Fiction sounds less natural. Long-form listening of 7+ hours is a stretch for many listeners.
    • Limits: eligibility rules change (language list, format rules, and the program is not worldwide in every case). Quality controls are thin: you can't direct "her voice should warm up in Chapter 9".
    • Check the current program page before planning around it.
  1. Microsoft Azure / Edge "read aloud" voices (free tier) Azure's free tier of neural text-to-speech is genuinely free for small volumes and produces high-quality voices. Not free for publishing at scale, and — important — Azure's terms do not grant you a blanket license to sell the audio as a retail audiobook product. Using it to proof-listen to your manuscript is a great, legal, free use. Turning it into a "for sale" MP3 is a licensing question — read the current terms.
  1. Apple Books digital narration Apple introduced digital narration for audiobooks in partnership with specific TTS providers, aimed mainly at established publishers; individual self-service access has been inconsistent, and availability varies by storefront. It's "free to hear on the store" but not a self-serve upload pipe for most indie authors today. Check what Apple published documents say this month.

The honest-limits section

Free AI narration has real limitations:

  • Audible/ACX will not accept AI-generated narration into its standard royalty pool today. An AI-narrated audiobook today means Google Play Books (and similar direct stores), not Audible. If Audible is your required store: AI narration is not your route yet.
  • Listeners can tell more often than AI demos suggest — especially fiction with dialogue, and especially across long listens. It's not an insult to the tech; it's just how ears work after 40 minutes.
  • Licensing reality: many "free" AI voice tools in consumer apps let you make the audio but don't clearly license you to sell it. When in doubt, the license agreement is the contract that matters, not the download button.
  • Emotional range: AI handles a steady guide-tone well (manuals, how-to, meditation scripts) and a real character interiority poorly. Nonfiction: good fit. Emotion-driven fiction: not yet.
  • A hybrid that actually works: DIY-record the book yourself with decent production values (this handbook), and use free AI narration only to proof-listen to your manuscript before recording — hearing an AI voice read your prose is a fantastic editor's tool for finding clunky sentences, and it costs nothing.

The honest AI verdict

For a genuinely-free, sellable audiobook in the world as it is today:

  • Nonfiction/manual/self-help: Google Play Books auto-narration is a legitimate, low-effort route to get some audio version of your book out — perfect for testing whether your audience wants an audiobook at all.
  • Fiction or prestige titles: AI narration is not there yet for you; DIY (Route 1) or hire (Route 2) make the final book genuinely good.
  • If Audible matters: DIY or hire. Today, full stop.

^ Also worth saying: expectations from AI-generated audio keep rising month over month, stores are renegotiating their policies, and some "insist on human narration" markets (like traditional library licensing) haven't settled their rules at all. Save yourself headaches: pick your route by the store you need — not by the tech you're tempted by.

The decision table

Need Best route
Budget $0–$150, have 2–6 spare evenings/week, want full control DIY (this handbook)
Book is a career-defining nonfiction title, budget $1k–$3k Hire
Memoir or the author's voice is the story DIY
Just want an audiobook version out fast and free as a test Google Play auto-narration
Must be in Audible royalty pool DIY or Hire
Have health/life constraints; solo recording infeasible Hire or AI
Fiction with lots of dialogue, character voices Hire (DIY works only if you enjoy it)

Mixing routes over time

These routes are not enemies. A common and very sane path:

  1. DIY-record your first book with this handbook (cheap, learn the craft).
  2. Hire a narrator for your next book (now you know exactly how to direct and clean a script for a pro — you've edited a whole book).
  3. Put an AI-narrated version of a backlist title on Google Play as a free experiment.

Whatever you choose, Chapter 3's spec sheet still describes the technical file you'd eventually want — because if you DIY later, or hire later, or even license an AI voice later, well-prepared audio never goes to waste.
