Listening, Distributing, and After You Finish

You have a folder full of spec-perfect MP3s. Congratulations — the hard part is done. This short chapter covers what's next: final quality checking, where to sell, and a note about the store landscape.

The final listen (one evening, well spent)

Before uploading anywhere, do one full "listener pass":

  • Export all MP3s into one folder, put them in a phone or computer player, and listen in order, at normal speed (1x — no skipping).
  • You're checking for continuity problems: a jump in loudness between sessions (should be fixed by mastering — verify it actually was), pacing drift, a leftover "sorry", a chapter that starts too abruptly.
  • Mark timestamps (photo of notes works). Good news: most little problems are best fixed by a single 20-minute pickup session per chapter, not a chapter re-record.
  • Bonus clues to listen for: consistent mouth clicks on certain words (add a green apple to the ritual), and one "recording day" that sounds different from the rest (usually a mic-distance or posture drift — a lesson to note for next time, not necessarily a re-record).

Where audiobooks are sold (2026 snapshot)

The store landscape has consolidated a lot and continues to shift. Here's the honest shape of it for indie authors:

Store / program How indies get in Notes
Amazon/Audible (ACX) Direct upload at acx.com, or via an audiobook distributor The dominant market. Strict specs (Chapter 3). Exclusive (Audible-only) contracts get you a bigger royalty pool; non-exclusive plans exist too
Spotify Normally through an audiobook distributor; direct-in options have come and gone as Spotify's audiobook programs evolve Spotify's audiobook offering is huge with listeners now; distribution models rotate, check current docs
Apple Books Through distributors, or (in a limited set of cases) direct via Apple's tooling Second most recognized marketplace
Google Play Books Direct via Partner Center (supports auto-narration; Chapter 6) The most AI-friendly major store

Two honest structural notes:

  1. ACX acceptance is its own bar. The Audible-side checker runs a fully automated pass when you upload. If your files follow Chapter 3 exactly, they sail through in most cases. When they fail, the error email names the file and the measurement — usually room tone or sample rate. Fix and re-upload; nothing is lost.
  2. Wide distribution goes through aggregators. One distributor account (audiobook-focused aggregators like Findaway's branches, Draft2Digital's audiobook side, or paid per-title services) pushes your files to Spotify/Apple/Google and others all at once. Fees and cuts vary by service and by year. Do your own quick comparison before you commit.

Our companion guide, Publishing Beyond KDP, covers the distribution ecosystem in depth. This handbook gives you files any distributor will happily accept.

Pricing (one paragraph, honestly)

Audiobook pricing is set more rigidly than ebook pricing: major programs want credit/listener models to hold, so "your price" is often a suggestion to a credit system at popular stores. For self-narrated nonfiction, $12.99–$21.99 list price is the common band for decent-length books, and you're often paid on sale price, not list, in some plans. Price it once, don't fuss — marketing for audiobooks is mostly about getting the first listen, not the tenth.

Launch checklist (happy path)

  1. All MP3s spec-check clean (Chapter 3 checklist run).
  2. One full listener pass done.
  3. Book cover exists at required store sizes (distributor pages tell you; 1600×1600 minimum is common for audiobooks).
  4. Book description written for ears — narrate it mentally; the audiobook store shows it in text, but your cover/description is the same persuasive job it was for the printed version.
  5. Accounts: eBooks/eBook distributor + audiobook store accounts ready.
  6. Metadata (title, series, narrator name = your name, ISBN decisions) decided once and reused identically everywhere.

Back matter and pre-order (the small wins)

  • Consider releasing the audiobook as a pre-order with a stated date once the book is genuinely close. A pre-order window (even a two-week one) is free visibility in store-new-release grids.
  • If you DIY-narrated, put "Read by the author" in the metadata; it's a genuine selling point and the store fields explicitly ask.
  • Keep your master WAVs (masters/ folder from Chapter 5) forever. Every future distributor or voice change will ask for them.

What to do with a life after finishing

Stats worth knowing (and calming): most audiobook sales for a mid-list title arrive over years, not weeks. The finish line here is "the book is for sale everywhere it can be". After that:

  • If sales come and listeners ask for more: your next book's audiobook is now 40% faster to produce. Plan on it.
  • If you hired or used auto-narration and a DIY version suddenly makes sense later: nothing from this handbook expires except the store-page screenshots.
  • If this was your trial run and the format isn't clicking at one store yet: audiobooks are a long shelf. Leave the book up; costs are zero after mastering.

You did the thing. Sleep well.
